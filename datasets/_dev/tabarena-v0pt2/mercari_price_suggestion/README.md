## In brief

**Question.** What price will a second-hand item sell for on the Mercari app?

**Use case.** When a seller lists an item on Mercari, the app suggests a price from the listing (the competition's stated aim).

**One row.** One item listing on Mercari, a shopping app for second-hand goods (1,481,612 listings); the place and period are not stated.

**Predicted.** The price the item sold for, in US dollars, on a log scale.

**Known at prediction time.** The item's name, condition, category, brand, whether the seller pays shipping, and the seller's free-text description.

**What the test data stands for.** Random listings, as in the competition: a split forced from temporal, since the listings came in over a period but carry no dates, so the test data stands for other listings of the same period, not later ones.

**Our changes to the source.** We drop the 874 listings priced $0, a price no sale has.

> **Use case fit: direct (the rows, features and split mirror the use case).** Everything the model sees is what a seller enters when listing, and the label is the price a suggestion should hit. The release has no dates, so the split cannot follow time.

## Curation notes

We start from the Kaggle competition dataset.

- We log1p scale the target; the metric is the competition's RMSLE on the price, computed after the inverse (`target_transform="log1p"`).
- The dataset was solved by Kaggle experts by tabular models and a lot of custom string preprocessing. So it is a great use case to test encoding pipelines! The dataset also has mostly string data.
- The task was treated and solved as an IID task on Kaggle.
- We replace "No description yet" with NaN.
- We take the full data (about 1.48M rows after de-duplication) with 3-fold cross-validation, so every fold trains on about 990k rows. Until 2026-10 a `mercari_price_suggestion_1m` version sub-sampled it to one 1M / 250k split.
- Ordinal scales (2026-10-30, the curator's marks of 2026-10-31 and 2026-11-01, CLEAN-14): 1 column is ordered
  categories in the order of the source's codebook (`ORDINAL_LEVELS`); 1 of them decoded from the source's codes
  into its words (`CODE_LABELS`, 2026-11-01).
- Codes decoded (2026-11-01, CLEAN-12, CLEAN-14; the curator's rule: a code whose codebook says in words what
  each level is carries the words, a score without them is a number): 1 ordered codes carry their codebook's
  words (`CODE_LABELS`), in the same order; 1 unordered codes carry their words: `shipping`.
- Price $0 (2026-11-09, Andrej): we drop the 874 listings priced $0 (0.06%), in `_clean` before the log, which
  leaves 1,481,612 listings. The competition calls the price "the price that the item was sold for", no sale is
  free, and no other listing is below $3. A public script of the competition drops them the same way
  (`train[train['price'] > 0]`, https://gist.github.com/pjankiewicz/8b0e9ea3238252c169574c524da67cde). Kept,
  each would sit about 8.4 squared log units from a typical prediction and carry about 2.6% of a good model's
  squared error, for a price no listing explains.
- Time basis: ForcedIIDFromTemporal: listings made over a period the host does not state, each price known at
  the sale; the app prices listings made later. Product mentions date the extract to 2017 before September
  (Galaxy S8 729 listings, fidget spinners 2,637, the iPhone 8 10 against 9,281 for the iPhone 7), but no
  listing has a date and train_id is a shuffle (every dated product flat across its tenths) (Time: Lost). Leak
  test across the split: 280 listings (0.02%) repeat another's fields and text, 0.01% of test rows have such a
  twin in training. Listing dates would allow a split by time.

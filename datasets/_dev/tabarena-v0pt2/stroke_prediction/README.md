## In brief

**Question.** Has a patient had a stroke, judged from a few facts of the health record?

**Use case.** A hospital chain wants "the probability of stroke happening to their patients", to help doctors "take proactive health measures" (the contest's wording); the label, though, records a stroke the patient has already had.

**One row.** One patient record of a hospital chain, from the McKinsey Analytics Online Hackathon of 2018 (43,400 patients; place and period not stated).

**Predicted.** Whether the patient has had a stroke (1.8%), at a time not stated, before or after the record.

**Known at prediction time.** Age, sex, high blood pressure, heart disease, average blood glucose and smoking status.

**What the test data stands for.** Random patients.

**Our changes to the source.** We use the contest's whole training file instead of the Kaggle cut, drop the body-mass index, whose missing values follow a stroke, and drop marriage, work type and residence, which carry nothing beyond age and look generated.

> **Use case fit: approximate (a stand-in with a known gap).** The record has no dates, so the label is a stroke at any time rather than within a fixed time after the record, and the facts may have been recorded at the stroke itself (a missing BMI follows the stroke); the period of the glucose average is not stated. Atrial fibrillation, a blood-pressure value, diabetes and cholesterol, inputs of clinical stroke scores, are missing.

## Curation notes

We start with train_ajEneEa.csv, the training file of the McKinsey Analytics Online Hackathon - Healthcare Analytics (Analytics Vidhya DataHack, 2018-04-14).

- Prediction point: a patient's record in a hospital chain's health records. The file has no dates and the label is "0 - no stroke, 1 - suffered stroke" (data dictionary), so the task is the contest's: estimate from the record, written before or after a stroke, whether the patient has had one. The facts may have been recorded at the stroke itself (see the bmi bullet), and the period over which the glucose level is averaged is not stated. Atrial fibrillation, a blood-pressure value, diabetes and cholesterol, inputs of clinical stroke scores (QStroke, the Framingham profile), are not in the file.
- Source: the record links fedesoriano's Kaggle "Stroke Prediction Dataset", a cut of this file (its first 249 stroke rows and first 4,861 others). We use the whole file: 8.5 times the rows and 3.1 times the strokes, at the contest's stroke share (first decision table).
- Partly generated or altered, dropped: Residence_type, ever_married and work_type carry nothing beyond age. For adults, a logistic regression on 1-year ages gains nothing from them (likelihood-ratio p = 0.47 for Residence_type, 0.39 for ever_married, 0.68 to 0.88 for the work types, against 7e-145 for sex), where real survey data show the dependence (NHANES 1999-2018, 35,850 adults: marriage depends on the other columns beyond age, p = 3e-52). Women and men are married alike at every age, 45.0% and 45.0% at 25-29 against 50.7% and 38.0% in NHANES (third decision table). We found no statement of how the file was made. The three columns add nothing to a model (regularised LightGBM ROC AUC 0.8505 with them, 0.8501 without, each of 9 splits within 0.002), so we drop them. What remains: the glucose level has no value below 55.00 and a spike at 65, not linked to the stroke (the spike at 65 is no larger among patients with a stroke than among those without, p = 0.45); kept.
- Leak, dropped: bmi. A missing BMI follows the stroke: with all other columns of the file fixed, a stroke multiplies the odds of a missing BMI by 3.68 (95% CI 2.98 to 4.54), in every age band (second decision table). One possible reason is a record made at the stroke admission, where no one weighed the patient. But among patients without a stroke a missing BMI becomes rarer with the Patient ID (6.9% to 0.3% across fifths of the id for those without hypertension or heart disease), and among strokes it does not (20.7% to 18.1%), which fits stroke rows assembled apart as well. The BMI values add nothing beyond the missingness (ROC AUC 0.847 without the column, 0.846 with the missing values drawn at random), so the whole column goes. Under LEAK-10, set apart from kick (a blank field after the outcome kept under LEAK-38): here the leak is measured in every age band, BMI is a named field, and the host of the contest was not the data owner.
- We drop the Patient ID (`id`), unique per row.
- Features as published: hypertension and heart_disease stay 0/1; gender and smoking_status are categories. The target maps 0/1 to No/Yes.
- Missing values: smoking_status is blank for 30.6% of the rows, all children under 10 among them. It is missing independently of the stroke (odds ratio 0.96), so it stays missing (the Kaggle cut writes "Unknown").
- Anomaly: ages under 2 are years plus 0.08 per month, and ages 78 to 82 hold more patients per year than 70 to 77, as if older ages were folded in. Kept as published.
- Duplicates: on the six shipped features, 198 rows repeat an earlier row, none with a stroke, and one pair of 76-year-old men shares every feature with different labels. No two rows of the raw file are the same beyond the id, so they are different patients and stay.
- IID split: one row per patient, and a model is used for new patients.
- Note: age alone scores ROC AUC 0.840 and a regularised LightGBM 0.850, above age on 9 of 9 splits (+0.010 on average). The untuned task probe (5 of the 9 splits) has its LightGBM at 0.823, below age alone at 0.832, and its linear model at 0.850; no task-probe flag.

### 2. bmi dropped: a missing BMI follows the stroke

In every age band, 16.5% to 20.8% of the strokes have no BMI, against 9.3% to 14.5% of the patients without a stroke but with hypertension or heart disease and 1.6% to 3.6% of the others. With age, sex, the comorbidities, glucose, smoking and the other columns fixed, a stroke multiplies the odds of a missing BMI by 3.7 (curation_comments, Leak).

### 3. Residence_type, ever_married and work_type dropped: they carry nothing beyond age

Women and men are married alike and hold the same work types at every age, and about half of every group is urban; in NHANES 1999-2018, 50.7% of women and 38.0% of men aged 25-29 were ever married (curation_comments, Partly generated). The three columns add nothing to a model, so they go.
